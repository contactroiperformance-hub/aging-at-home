What is a walk-in shower?

A walk-in shower is any shower you can step into without climbing over a tub wall — but the term covers very different designs, and the differences matter for safety, cost, and access:

Low-threshold shower An entry lip of a few inches or less — the most common accessible upgrade.
Curbless shower No lip at all: the floor slopes to the drain, so nothing interrupts the entry.
Doorless shower An open entry with no glass door — easy access and caregiver assistance, needs water-containment planning.
Roll-in shower A curbless design with the entry width, space, controls, and seating planned for wheelchair use.
Standard shower marketed as “walk-in” Some ordinary stalls carry the label without accessibility features — check the threshold height and dimensions, not the name.

Note the last one: not everything marketed as “walk-in” is accessible, and no walk-in shower is automatically wheelchair accessible — that depends on the entry width, turning space, controls, and seating of the full installed design.

Who may consider a walk-in shower?

People who have difficulty stepping over a bathtub wall Those who prefer showering rather than bathing Anyone who wants seating or handheld controls Households needing space for caregiver assistance Homeowners planning ahead for changing mobility

When transfers or mobility needs are complex, an assessment by an occupational therapist or similar professional helps match the design to the person. This page is general information, not medical advice.

Safety and accessibility features

Low or no threshold Properly anchored grab bars Built-in or fold-down seating Handheld showerhead Reachable controls Slip-resistant surfaces Adequate lighting Anti-scald temperature controls Sufficient entry width Correct drainage and water containment

Individual accessories don’t automatically make the complete bathroom accessible — features work when they’re chosen and positioned for the person using them.

Walk-in shower options

Prefabricated low-threshold shower Kit base and wall panels in a standard footprint — the fastest, most economical path.
Custom tiled walk-in shower Any size or design, built on site with full waterproofing and tile work.
Curbless shower Zero-entry design with a recessed, sloped floor — the smoothest possible entry.
Doorless shower Open layout without glass hardware; best in larger showers with planned drainage.
Shower with built-in seat Bench or fold-down seat integrated from the start, with reinforced walls.
Wheelchair-accessible / roll-in design Curbless entry plus the space, controls, and seating for seated or wheeled use.

Walk-in shower vs. tub-to-shower conversion

They overlap: a tub-to-shower conversion describes the renovation process — removing a tub — while a walk-in shower describes the resulting shower type. If you’re replacing an existing tub, start with the conversion cost guide.

Walk-in shower vs. walk-in tub

A shower prioritizes easy entry, open space, and quick everyday bathing; a walk-in tub preserves seated soaking at the cost of fill-and-drain time. Neither is right for everyone — the full comparison is in Walk-In Tub vs. Walk-In Shower.

Frequently asked questions

What is considered a walk-in shower?

Any shower you enter without climbing over a tub wall — from a simple low-threshold stall to a fully curbless, roll-in design. The label alone tells you little; the threshold height, entry width, and interior space determine how accessible it actually is.

Is a walk-in shower safer than a bathtub?

It removes the tallest step in most homes — the tub wall — which is a meaningful improvement for many people. Overall safety still depends on grab bars, seating, slip-resistant flooring, and lighting chosen for the specific user. This is general guidance, not medical advice.

How much does a walk-in shower cost?

A typical planning budget is roughly $6,000–$12,000 nationally, with basic prefab installations below that and custom curbless projects reaching $20,000 or more. Our full cost guide breaks down every driver — these are planning ranges, not quotes.

Can a walk-in shower fit in a small bathroom?

Usually yes. Compact bases, corner layouts, and curtain entries are designed for small rooms, and converting the existing tub alcove is the most common approach of all.

Does a walk-in shower need a door?

No. Doorless and curtain designs work well and make caregiver assistance easier — provided the layout and drainage genuinely contain water. Doorless designs favor larger showers.

What is the difference between curbless and low-threshold showers?

A low-threshold shower keeps a small entry lip — typically an inch or a few. A curbless shower has none: the floor itself slopes to the drain. Curbless is the smoothest entry but requires floor and drainage work, which is why it costs more.

Can a walk-in shower accommodate a wheelchair?

Yes, when designed for it — a roll-in shower needs a curbless entry, adequate width and turning space, reachable controls, and appropriate seating. No shower is wheelchair accessible by label alone, and compliance claims require the full installed design to be evaluated against the applicable requirements.

How long does installation usually take?

Prefab installations often take a few days; custom tiled projects a week or more; curbless remodels longer when floor structure or permits are involved. Ask your installer to confirm a timeline after assessing your bathroom.

Does Medicare cover a walk-in shower?

Original Medicare covers defined medical services and qualifying durable medical equipment; permanent bathroom remodeling is not listed as standard covered DME. Some Medicare Advantage plans offer supplemental benefits that vary by plan and year — verify with your specific plan.

Can Medicaid or VA benefits help with the project?

Sometimes. Medicaid home- and community-based programs in some states cover home accessibility modifications, and certain VA programs assist eligible veterans with medically necessary modifications. Eligibility varies and approval is never guaranteed — see our financial assistance guide.
