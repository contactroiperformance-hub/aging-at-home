How Much Does a Walk-In Tub Cost?

A complete walk-in tub project commonly requires a planning budget of approximately $4,000 to $15,000, including the unit and professional installation, based on Angi’s 2026 cost data. Basic tubs and straightforward replacements may fall below this range; premium brands, wheelchair-accessible doors, larger models, therapeutic systems, and extensive bathroom modifications can push the total above $20,000, per This Old House’s 2026 research.

There is no universal average: some sources price a standard tub swap, while others include broader labor and modifications. That’s why quotes for the “same” project can differ by thousands — and why itemized comparisons matter more here than in almost any other home project.

Basic soaking tub $2,500–$5,000 Simple soaker, straightforward replacement in the existing footprint.
Standard installed walk-in tub $4,000–$8,000 Mid-line unit with seat, grab points, and professional installation.
Hydrotherapy or air-jet tub $7,000–$12,000 Therapy systems plus the electrical work they usually require.
Wheelchair-accessible tub $8,000–$15,000 Wider outward door built for seated transfers.
Premium / heavily customized $15,000–$20,000+ Large or two-seat models, extensive bathroom modifications.

How we build these figures: published estimates differ because sources use different project scopes, contractor databases, material assumptions, labor markets, bathroom configurations, installation requirements, and geographic samples. We anchor on Angi’s 2026 installed range ($4,000 to $15,000) and use This Old House’s wider 2026 research for the below-range basic configurations and $20,000+ premium scenarios. Tub cost versus installed cost

The single biggest source of confusion in walk-in tub pricing: the unit and the project are different numbers. A complete installed price typically includes:

Equipment Delivery Removal of old tub Installation labor Plumbing Electrical Wall surround Finish work Permits Disposal

A quote that looks unusually low often covers the equipment only. Always ask which of these lines are included before comparing offers.

Door configuration

Inward-swinging doors are the most common: the water pressure helps seal them, and they need no clearance outside the tub — but the door can’t open until the tub drains, and it swings into the bather’s space during entry. Outward-swinging doors open even with water present and give a clearer entry, but need free space beside the tub and typically cost more. Wider wheelchair-accessible openings are built for seated transfers and carry the largest premium.

No configuration is safe for every user — the right choice depends on how the specific person enters, turns, and transfers. Test the motion in a showroom or with an installer before committing.

Installation factors

Bathroom dimensions Existing tub footprint Doorway access for delivery Floor strength Drain and supply lines Water-heater capacity Electrical needs Fill and drain speed Wall surrounds Bathroom ventilation

Two of these surprise buyers most often: jetted and heated models usually need a dedicated electrical circuit, and a filled two-seat tub is heavy enough that older floors sometimes need reinforcement.

Optional features

Practical accessibility features

Anti-scald controls Properly anchored grab bars Slip-resistant floor Handheld shower Fast drain Quick-fill faucet

Comfort and spa features

Heated seat Air jets Water jets Chromotherapy lighting Aromatherapy and audio packages

Budget the practical column first. Comfort features are legitimate — hydrotherapy is a real reason people choose these tubs — but they’re where quotes inflate fastest, and each one adds equipment, electrical, and service complexity.

Water-heater considerations

Walk-in tubs are deep, and a comfortable soak means filling most of that depth with warm water — often more than a standard tank delivers in one go. There’s no universal size recommendation: the right capacity depends on the tub’s volume, your current heater, and household usage. Have the installer evaluate capacity before purchase; a heater upgrade discovered after installation is the classic unbudgeted expense on these projects.

Walk-in tub brands and quote-only pricing

Many major walk-in tub brands don’t publish complete installed prices. That’s partly legitimate — installed cost genuinely depends on your bathroom — and partly a sales model built around in-home consultations, where pricing can be flexible and discounts appear when you hesitate. We don’t rank brands or estimate brand-specific prices here without a documented methodology. Protect yourself the boring way: multiple itemized quotes, no same-day-only pricing, and time to compare.

Financial assistance overview

Original Medicare generally does not list walk-in tubs as standard durable medical equipment. Some Medicare Advantage plans may offer supplemental home-safety benefits that vary by plan and year. Medicaid home-modification programs vary by state, and certain VA programs may assist eligible veterans with medically necessary bathroom modifications. Approval is never guaranteed — confirm directly with each program.

Frequently asked questions

What does a walk-in tub cost with installation?

Angi’s 2026 data puts a complete installed project at roughly $4,000–$15,000 as a national planning range. This Old House’s research shows basic configurations can start below that, while premium and heavily customized projects exceed $20,000. Your bathroom and market set the real number.

What is the cheapest type of walk-in tub?

A basic soaking model installed in the existing tub footprint — planning around $2,500–$5,000 installed. No jets means no electrical work, which keeps both installation and lifetime service costs down.

Why are walk-in tubs so expensive?

You’re buying a sealed, engineered appliance — watertight door, seat, safety systems, often pumps and heaters — plus skilled installation involving plumbing and frequently electrical work. Quote-only sales models with in-home consultations also add sales cost into the price.

Does a walk-in tub require electrical work?

Basic soakers usually don’t. Jetted, heated, and fast-drain models typically need a dedicated circuit installed by an electrician — ask every bidder whether electrical work is included in the quote.

Will I need a larger water heater?

Possibly. Deep tubs can outrun a standard tank’s hot-water delivery. There’s no universal size answer — have the installer evaluate your heater against the specific tub’s fill volume before you buy.

How long does installation take?

A straightforward replacement in the existing footprint often takes one to three days. Electrical work, wall surrounds, plumbing changes, or repairs found during removal extend that.

Does Medicare pay for a walk-in tub?

Original Medicare generally does not list walk-in tubs as standard durable medical equipment. Some Medicare Advantage plans offer supplemental home-safety benefits that vary by plan and year — verify with your specific plan. See our dedicated Medicare guide for the full picture.

Can Medicaid pay for a walk-in tub?

In some states, Medicaid home- and community-based programs cover home accessibility modifications, which may include bathing modifications. Eligibility, covered projects, and limits vary by state and program — confirm with your state Medicaid agency.

Can VA benefits help pay for a walk-in tub?

Certain VA programs — notably HISA grants — may assist eligible veterans with medically necessary bathroom modifications, subject to eligibility and medical justification. SAH and SHA housing grants are separate programs with their own rules. A Veterans Service Officer can help you apply.

Is a walk-in tub worth the cost?

For people who genuinely love baths, can enter safely through the door, and accept the fill-and-drain wait, owners tend to be satisfied. If the goal is simply safer bathing, a walk-in shower or tub-to-shower conversion often delivers that for less — compare both paths before deciding.

How much do walk-in tub repairs cost?

It varies by component: door-seal replacements are the most common repair, while pump, heater, and control repairs on jetted models cost more. The practical protections are a strong warranty, a clear service plan, and choosing only the features you’ll actually use.

Should I buy directly from a manufacturer or a contractor?

Both models can work. Manufacturer-direct programs bundle the unit and installation with one warranty point; independent contractors may offer more model flexibility and competitive labor. Either way, insist on itemized pricing and compare at least two or three offers.
